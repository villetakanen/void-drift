# Writing a study

## Give each opening element a job

The title identifies the subject. The question names the decision being investigated.
The opening supplies the concrete situation and reasoning needed to understand it.
Read these together and merge repeated framing. Retain constraints and uncertainty
even when that requires more words.

### Example: stellar encounters

These excerpts illustrate an editorial choice in the 11 November 2026 opening;
they do not prescribe wording or a layout for other subjects.

Before (question excerpt shortened):

> **Title:** Stellar encounters: different stars, different decisions
>
> **Question:** How should different kinds of stars create recognisably different situations…?
>
> **Section:** A new star asks for a different approach
>
> **Opening:** Approaching an unfamiliar star should invite recognition and a decision…

Each line largely repeats the premise. The concrete design constraints arrive later.

After (illustrative opening excerpt):

> **Title:** Stellar encounters
>
> **Question:** How should star types change how a pilot approaches, collects energy and escapes?
>
> **01 / The standard Sun as a reference**
>
> The standard Sun offers energy collection while threatening the ship through
> gravitational pull, exposure and terminal contact. Additional star types should
> create different combinations of opportunity and danger that a pilot can
> recognize and respond to.
>
> Real stellar relationships guide these differences, with distances and timescales
> compressed for arcade play. Which star types to include, how they behave and
> which cues communicate their behaviour remain open.

The title names the subject; the question identifies the pilot decisions at stake;
the opening introduces the existing encounter, intended differences and unresolved
choices. The comparison conditions and scope boundaries remain in the following
text. This excerpt is not a complete replacement for the problem statement.

## Replace generic promises with design information

Explain the intended quality, relevant constraints or unresolved decision. If a
sentence only announces that the study will investigate, explore or define its
subject, remove it. Keep plans that explain how a specific uncertainty will be
investigated.

### Example: energy support

Before, from the energy-support study's problem statement:

> This study will establish the device’s nature and defining characteristics,
> then derive its name and shape from them. It is a static object mounted above
> or below a ship, built from readable solid parts and flat planes.

The first sentence announces an investigation without explaining the design
problem. The second supplies actual constraints.

After (illustrative excerpt, retaining the surrounding problem's intended role):

> The device must read as stored energy while mounted above or below a ship.
> Its static construction must communicate that role through solid parts and
> flat planes. Its name and shape remain open.

The revision states the intended reading, construction constraints and unresolved
choices directly. The following text still supplies the fleet and viewing scales
and keeps charging rules outside the inquiry.

## Preserve the reason behind a requirement

Keep the concrete condition, its consequence and the reasoning that connects them
to the proposed change. Remove repetition while retaining the information a future
designer would need to reconsider the decision.

### Example: plasma charging feedback

Useful reasoning from the plasma-light study:

> During the charge it paints no light, so on the ship a charging engine and a
> dead one look the same; only a line of text in the deck’s gutter reports the
> charge.

An overly compressed rewrite (invented to illustrate the loss):

> Plasma needs a visible charging effect.

That loses the observed ambiguity, where it occurs and why the existing feedback
is insufficient. A future designer inherits a requirement without its cause.

A useful edit:

> On the ship, a charging plasma engine looks identical to an inactive one.
> Only the flight deck’s gutter text reports charging. The engine needs visible
> charging feedback so its state can be read on the ship itself.

The original and the useful edit both explain the design problem. Brevity alone
does not make an edit better; preserve the explanation during editing.

## State the extent of a decision

Record which properties were accepted, why, and which remain provisional. A
successful example may establish one quality while leaving its other properties
open. Preserve those boundaries when summarizing the study.

### Example: utility family accent

Useful reasoning from the utility-machinery study's round 02.4 (excerpt shortened):

> A distinct, vivid family colour carried by selected physical parts is a useful
> identity cue. […] This is an accepted direction for family recognition.
>
> The exact hue, its placement and the additional family cues remain open. Amber
> is a successful example, not a selected colour.

An overly broad summary (invented to illustrate the loss):

> The family’s colour language is established.

That obscures what a future designer can preserve and what they can still explore.

A useful summary:

> The family uses a vivid accent on recurring physical parts. At this round, the
> hue and placement remain open; amber demonstrates the direction without settling
> the colour.

The retained principle is family recognition through an accent on physical parts.
The example's hue and placement remain available for exploration at this point in
the study. Later decisions can settle them; this summary describes round 02.4.

## Close the study by preserving its findings and retiring its apparatus

A closed study explains the decision and retains useful evidence without requiring
its temporary tools to run. The main skill's closing procedure owns capture and
code cleanup.

An illustrative closure excerpt for a button comparison:

> **Chosen:** brackets in the pane’s hue. They make quiet buttons recognizable
> without competing with the primary action.
>
> **Evidence:** a static comparison of the title menu and Hangar launch row.
>
> **Still provisional:** bracket brightness.

This illustrates the form, not the button study's final decision or an existing
capture. The actual record embeds the retained comparison and states its relevant
conditions. After replacing the temporary comparison with that record, remove its
unused study-specific controls and component code. An interactive example can remain
for a named continuing purpose; the record explains why it is still needed.
