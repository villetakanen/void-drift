## 01 / A winding with weight

A Gravity coil mounted beside an engine should look like deliberately built technical machinery before any effect appears. A toroid, coil or spring supplies its physical identity: repeated solid windings, space between them, and a convincing structure holding them to the ship. The first question was which construction gives that identity weight and clarity in the game’s top-down view.

Outside a gravity field, the inactive opening and winding must distinguish it from an engine or energy store. Enlarged, the assembly should reveal purposeful parts; at flight scale, its principal masses and gaps must carry the same idea. Flat facets, upper-left light and restrained material contrast connect it to existing support modules. Technical character must come from construction, not fine lines that disappear when the ship shrinks.

The physical parts remain static, including when active. Like an engine, the coil produces separate FX: field interaction or exotic physics on the FX layer, with no animated mechanical parts. After the physical object, the intended next question was how its active FX within a gravity field interact with engine effects. Effect shape, rhythm and overlap remained unexplored; charging rules and flight balance were outside this initial inquiry.

## 02 / Construction before effects

<details>
<summary>02.1 / What space does the winding enclose? — findings</summary>

A closed toroid concentrates the machine around one aperture; an axial spring spreads its winding along a direction. The static sketches used the same cradle, materials and approximate envelope, with broad lit faces and dark side faces describing solids.

<figure>
  <img src="/studies/gravity-coil-2026-11-03/021-forms.png" alt="Closed toroid and axial spring sketches, enlarged and at native scale" width="1056" height="484" />
  <figcaption>Retirement capture · same cradle and materials, static and without FX. Small samples use the corrected 1× presentation, not the original incorrect 0.65×.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Finding:** these examples did not communicate in the game’s visual language and could not settle toroid versus spring. A support component must have no base or background plate: it fails beneath a ship and makes the object look like an isometric icon. Too much detail makes the component messy at 1×. This was a useful learning step, not a construction decision or an error to delete; neither construction was selected.

</details>

<details>
<summary id="density">02.2 / How few sections can carry the coil? — B preferred</summary>

Four broad winding sections were compared with eight narrower ones using the same aperture, outside dimensions, two attachment tabs and material. There is no backing surface; the void shows through the centre and gaps. The view is directly overhead, with bevels describing thickness.

</details>

<details>
<summary id="forms">02.3 / Nine constructions from the segmented ring</summary>

The retained B geometry restored yellow connectors. All nine written proposals carried forward substantial separate parts, open gaps, no backing plate and static construction. In longitudinal forms, the axis runs fore–aft: the overhead camera sees the spring’s side.

<figure>
  <img src="/studies/gravity-coil-2026-11-03/023-reference.png" alt="Retained eight-section ring with yellow connectors" width="1056" height="417" />
  <figcaption>Retirement capture · B geometry with yellow connectors restored. Construction reference only.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Segmented toroid:** eight faceted sectors around one open centre, with two short yellow side connectors; the compact reference.
2. **Elongated toroid:** stretch the ring fore–aft, retaining eight sections and waist connectors; a longer silhouette with one aperture.
3. **Split horseshoe:** open the aft end into space, ending in thick yellow terminals; gives the machine direction.
4. **Twin rings:** two smaller toroids joined fore–aft by a yellow bridge; two centres and a narrow waist, with Potential Well resemblance requiring scrutiny.
5. **Three-turn barrel spring:** a thick longitudinal helical band between yellow collars, with generous gaps and darker returning facets.
6. **Segmented helix:** six chunky curved pieces and recessed joints between yellow clamps; carries B’s separate-part character into a spiral.
7. **Tapered spring:** three heavy turns decreasing toward aft, capped by collars; directional but risks reading as an engine nozzle.
8. **Paired counter-wound springs:** two opposite longitudinal helices with separate clamps and a dark channel, without a shared plate.
9. **Spaced induction hoops:** three thick transverse hoops held by yellow side connectors; raised crowns and dark returns test discrete hoops against a continuous helix at 1×.

These were construction proposals, not selected designs. Continuity, depth and 1× readability still needed visual evidence.

</details>

<details>
<summary id="machines">02.5 / Supports that complete the machine — findings</summary>

Three arrangements per retained form varied yellow forks, inner structures and open braces while keeping white–charcoal working parts unchanged. Toroid, horseshoe and spring occupy successive rows. No arrangement has a base plate.

<figure>
  <img src="/studies/gravity-coil-2026-11-03/025-machines.png" alt="Nine support arrangements shown enlarged, free, underwing and on chassis" width="1056" height="1605" />
  <figcaption>Retirement capture · unchanged working shapes, speculative free/underwing/on-chassis contexts and 1× samples. Static supports only.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Findings:** the three contexts exposed different failures. A free-standing module must connect its own parts; the hull cannot supply missing structure. External toroid brace 3, horseshoe side struts 6 and spring arrangements left unsupported or floating-looking pieces. Inner bearing ring 2 added too much detail and too many gaps. Yellow lines alone read as schematic marks, not solid machinery.

All three springs remained unconvincing as enlarged parts-list objects even where 1× silhouettes suggested useful ideas. Supports and connectors needed their own volume and the same upper-left lighting as working parts. **No support arrangement was selected.**

</details>

<details>
<summary id="solid-machines">02.6 / Convincing graphic form — findings</summary>

The first attempt repeated the earlier failure: isometric-looking decorative icon art, with raised blocks and offset extrusions, made the equipment bulky and literal. Those drawings had already been removed. The intended direction was sleek, streamlined science fiction through restrained graphic forms.

The equipment needs to look convincing, not physically realistic. Connections should read as deliberate paths, without explaining a mechanically complete assembly. A few chosen planes can suggest volume and character. Connection, weight and belonging to the ship are the criteria; literal metal, plastic, bevels and structural detail are not requirements.

The retained toroid, horseshoe and spring supplied working shapes. The replacement would add only enough graphic structure to connect them, preserving silhouettes and open spaces. Upper-left light remained a volume cue, not permission for an isometric viewpoint. Free, underwing and on-chassis views remained the comparison.

</details>

<details>
<summary id="graphic-machines">02.7 / Connection through graphic form — selections</summary>

Narrow ribbons, swept paths and quiet charcoal returns suggested continuity without raised blocks or offset extrusions. Added structure should connect the working shapes without becoming the main subject. These were aesthetic proposals, not mechanical diagrams; free-standing, under-hull and on-chassis views tested one purposeful piece of equipment.

**Review decision:** the graphic direction was accepted for continued exploration. Carry forward **1 Inner ribbon, 2 Swept connection, 4 Inner return and 6 Terminal sweep**. Spring options 7–9 were dropped from this comparison. Options 3 and 5 remained references, not selected directions.

<summary id="belonging">02.10 / Whole equipment, part of the ship — findings</summary>

Longitudinal rails, a continuous open body and paired windings sharing a spine tested where completeness could come from. Each kept an opening and a few substantial parts. 1A showed connected structure could help, but its circular surround was not a requirement. Real Wasp and Fighter contexts prevented isolated appeal from standing in for belonging.

**Finding:** all three were acceptable under wing but remained floating icons over the chassis, like 1A. Supporting construction did not resolve belonging. The Potential Well appeared to work in both configurations. **1A / Inner ribbon remained the reference.**

<details>
<summary id="seating">02.11 / What makes equipment feel seated? — findings</summary>

The Potential Well has dark working interiors, whereas the coil opening reveals the hull. Whether the hull visibly holds the equipment was another hypothesis, not an established cause. The unchanged reference, contained interior and captured terminals were compared with the production Potential Well. Both ships appeared above hull; Wasp also appeared under wing, separating mounting relationship from chassis choice.

**Finding:** B read as a special well or window; C made the mismatch more pronounced. Neither explained the stylistic problem. 1A still looked good isolated and over space, but read as an icon on the chassis.

<details>
<summary id="planes">02.12 / A symbol or a surface? — findings</summary>

The ship uses a few broad planes; 1A repeats a small dark-edged object eight times. Keeping ring identity, opening and ribbon, B gave each sector one full face; C treated the winding as one folded surface without repeated divisions. Unchanged 1A and the production Potential Well were controls. No seating hardware or filled opening was added.

<details>
<summary id="construction">02.13 / Winding, housing and ship — findings</summary>

The ring, opening and yellow connections stayed fixed. B tested shared material language with identical geometry. C assigned the sectors a dark working-interior role and the light material a retaining role. B improving would support material continuity; C improving beyond B would support the housing/working-surface relationship. Neither needed to reopen the silhouette.

**Finding:** B and C both improved fit, but neither resolved it. Intended materials were ship and engine colours with safety-yellow supports under starlight. Palette and how light describes faces remained possible causes.

C used the same colours but lit the winding as one assembly: outside slopes and inside walls cannot face the light in the same direction. Yellow structure followed the same light.

<h3 id="assembly">02.15 / What holds the winding together?</h3>

The promising C winding stayed intact. A kept C as reference; B placed plates on a continuous carrier visible at the edge and through gaps; C captured the same plates between inner and outer supports joined by yellow retaining bridges. The question was whether visible support makes the coil complete and fitted, or turns it back into an outlined icon. Fighter, engines, mount and lighting stayed fixed.

## 03 / Readiness not reached

The original page left this part “To be developed after 02”. No readiness assessment or delivery scope was recorded, and no part 04 was present. The visual belonging question remained unresolved; isolated success and improved lighting had not established a finished fitting.

Still open were the final construction, facets and carrier relationship, fitted size and clearance, the cause of the chassis mismatch, and the later FX/engine interaction. The stopped record supplies references and negative findings, not production authorization. No study proposal is promoted here, and no owning spec or production behaviour is changed.
