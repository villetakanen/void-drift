## Question

What should a single, standard path instrument fix, and what may a study vary, so motion results from different studies can be compared?

Studies of ship velocity, mass or engine thrust all ask how one configuration moves compared with another. A comparison only means something if both were measured the same way. When each study invents its own scales, input sequence, graphs and framing, results from different studies cannot be set side by side, and often the two halves of a single study cannot either.

The instrument was framed as a fixed way of measuring. It calculates paths and draws them as static pictures, with no flying, playback or animation. Studies supply the configurations and do not redefine the measurement. Paths and their sources can vary; views, scales, units and input conventions stay the same wherever the instrument appears. With two studies a month apart, one varying engine thrust and one varying ship mass, both would draw three vehicles’ paths, speed, acceleration and turning acceleration on identical axes, so a reader could place a thrust result beside a mass result and see which matters more.

The instrument was required to be:

- **Standard.** Measurement choices belong to the instrument.
- **A Lab tool.** Full screen, with configurations chosen and variables tried in one collapsible side pane that keeps the data on stage.
- **Embeddable.** It treats its container as a screen at least as large as an iPad Pro. In the Lab it fills the container; in a study it takes the full container width and snaps to the center of the flowing text as the reader scrolls.

A measurement must be fixed enough to compare, and an instrument flexible enough to be useful across questions. Simulating flight, engine tuning and production values stayed outside the study.

## Results judged useful

**02.1.** Drawing two studies on one fixed set of scales makes them comparable; fitting axes per study does not. Two small studies ran the same calculation: one varied acceleration, the other the speed ceiling. The calculation was the existing free-rotation path for the “2 aft + 2 side” fixture on the shared 14-second input; the drawings were static.

![Two studies drawn with axes fitted to each study, then on one fixed instrument](/studies/path-instrument-2026-11-01/comparison-rows.png)

*02.1, reconstruction from current code, captured 24 November 2026. Study A varies acceleration (100, 175 and 250 u/s²); Study B varies the speed ceiling (150, 250 and 400 u/s). Top row: each study fits its own axes. Bottom row: both on the one fixed instrument. In the bottom row a value runs off the fixed range in places.*

The fixed ranges in this example were provisional, and the example could not say how a reader would feel about them.

**02.2.** The path view has two formats, switched by a toggle. One auto-zooms to show every path in the run. The other uses the instrument’s default scale, which is the same in every study, and lets the reader move around it with the mouse (dragging) or the keyboard (arrow buttons and Recenter). In default scale the view is larger than the screen can hold, so paths run off the edge and the reader pans to follow them. Both formats worked as they were. Showing every path, or showing them on a standard scale that is easier to understand, is enough for the path view, and it does not need more fidelity. This applies to the path view only. The default scale value was not decided. The study also did not decide a zoom control or how the trace views follow a pan.

**02.3.** A time slider under the path area sets a moment in time. A marker appears on every vehicle’s path, a cursor line crosses the speed, acceleration and turn traces and the input strip, and a table gives the values at that moment. It works like the cursor of a measuring tool and not a playback control: nothing moves until the reader moves it. The slider works for following the vehicles across the views, and both a tenth and a hundredth of a second are useful steps. A thin tick at 90° to the path at each marker shows the path’s direction at that moment; moving the slider shows which way the vehicle travels along it. In default scale a marker can sit off screen until the view is panned to it. The slider follows the shared range-input pattern the studies already used; turning it into a design-system component was left as a candidate for after the study.

## Not settled

Trace scaling, how configurations and path sources are chosen, the contents of the side pane, and whether the composed instrument reads well on an iPad and in the Lab. The fixed scales for the speed, acceleration and turn traces, and the numbers chosen in 02.1, remained provisional. Container behaviour, the single side pane and snapping into place were given in the brief, not investigated. A shell drafted after 02.3 was not shown on the study page.
