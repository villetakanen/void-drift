## Outcome / Failed in exploration

The designer closed the study after 02.10: “we are looping. close study as failed”. No complete sky was chosen for *Fires in the Sky*. Exploration ended before readiness or scope.

The photographic flight established two qualities the designer wanted to keep: a dark pocket or bright patch the eye can follow during a coast, and a nearer pair of lights with staggered gleams. The usable pan depended on what was moving. Those findings apply to the examples below. The study left the relationship between individual grains and a continuous field, the star cores and the form of the gleam unresolved.

## 01 / What does a pilot see as starlight?

For *Fires in the Sky*, the release question above comes down to how an individual light belongs to a field of stars. Today a repeatable field of white discs covers the flight view and pans and zooms with the arena. What should a star look like at flight scale? Where many distant stars gather, the pilot might see separate grains, a field of unresolved light with dark pockets, or a place where grains become a field. A nearer sun might briefly throw an optical cross. What would make that read as a gleam?

These choices belong to one sky. A quiet coast gives the pilot time to notice how an individual light belongs to the larger field. The next burn shows whether the ship and its cues still read. The Milky Way supplies an observed reference. Void Drift’s hi-fi low-fi direction asks how much the game can express through economical marks, composed light and their movement.

**Working conditions.** Keep the Sun-like share at 6% in the generated star overlay. The photographed population is uncontrolled. Aim for 2–5 recognizable lights in a view. The photographic departure established that a dark pocket or bright patch gives the eye something to follow, and that a nearer pair with gleam works in that flight. Pan and appearance are judged together. The balance of grains and continuous light, the star cores and the drawing of the gleam remain open.

<details id="other-suns">
<summary>02.3 / Our sun seen from somewhere else</summary>

From the Wasp, the local sun has a body and a surrounding field. It changes how the pilot flies. Imagine looking back at this arena from another system. The whole encounter would be too small to see, yet its sun could still be one light in somebody else’s sky.

**Proposed intention.** During a quiet coast, it might occur to the pilot that one of the distant lights is another sun like the one they just flew past. The release’s planned new ranked systems give that thought a place in *Fires in the Sky*. The 6% Sun-like population gives it an occasional subject. The pilot can keep flying without identifying or counting stars.

**Fly the departure.** Play B1 in Follow from zero. The local sun starts below the Wasp. During the coast, watch the smaller body cross a light below and to the left of the ship, around 2½ seconds. The light borrows its colour from the local sun. Its position on the distant plane stays fixed as Venus covers and uncovers it.

The crossing is deliberately staged. Four light-years or farther is the imagined distance. Whether the returning light reads as another sun is open.

**Finding.** A few individually recognizable specks of light make sense as distant suns. A pilot can pick one out during a coast. When many marks asked for that attention in 02.1, the sky became the focus.

**Range proposed for comparison.** One to ten identifiable lights seemed plausible at this step. How many the pilot might see together or come to recognize over a stretch of flight is still open. The 6% Sun-like share applies to the generated population.

<details id="how-many">
<summary>02.4 / How much company should that sun have?</summary>

One light gives the pilot a particular sun to recognize when Venus uncovers it. A few more might make it feel like one system among several. At some point each extra light might simply draw attention away from the Wasp.

In these opening frames the flight, star positions and Sun-like population are identical. Compare [one prominent light](/studies/distant-flight-sky-2026-11-18/count-1.png), [four](/studies/distant-flight-sky-2026-11-18/count-4.png) and [ten](/studies/distant-flight-sky-2026-11-18/count-10.png).

**Study decision.** Aim for **2–5 individually recognizable lights in a view**. At four, a second warm point suggested that the first belonged to a family. At ten, a near-vertical row drew the eye into tracing a pattern. Placement matters as well as count. The comparison showed one, four and ten; the endpoints of the chosen range are design judgments. The generated field retains its 6% Sun-like share.

**Established reference.** The pilot sees the Milky Way from within it. The earlier whole-sky comparison established the need for structure across the field. The 2–5 recognizable lights serve as individual accents within that larger composition.

</details>

<details id="milky-way-edge">
<summary>02.5 / Flying along the edge of the Milky Way</summary>

**Question.** Which qualities of the Milky Way give the whole field structure while the Wasp is moving?

**Hypothesis.** A partial view of the Milky Way can suggest its scale. Dark gaps cut into a broad reach of unresolved starlight, giving it a broken edge the eye can follow beyond the frame. The 2–5 identifiable suns remain individual points within that larger shape.

The B1 departure put a broken edge behind Venus as the Wasp coasted at two seconds and burned again at three.

**Motion finding.** The broad grey edge made the slow camera response visible. Its outline stayed almost in place while Venus swept past, and the sky looked stuck. The sparse lights in 02.4 did not expose that relationship. This observation belongs to the edge in motion; the scroll rate for a finished sky remains open.

**Appearance feedback.** The edge read as “grey goo.” It was drawn as a continuous wash to stand in for the combined light of many distant stars. Switching the wash on and off showed how much space that shape occupied behind the Wasp. The example gave the pilot little visual evidence that the light came from stars, so it could not settle how the Milky Way should read in flight.

<details id="sky-travel">
<summary>02.6 / How much should the grey edge travel?</summary>

**Question.** As Venus sweeps across the view, when does the grey edge begin to feel like a distant part of the scene moving past the Wasp?

**Hypothesis.** A stronger response to the camera should let the pilot follow a bend in the edge through the coast while Venus still travels visibly faster. The earlier four-light comparison lost that bend and could not expose the difference.

**Finding at roughly 1×.** In the B1 departure, even 45% camera travel likely leaves the grey edge moving too little. The 4.5%, 20% and 45% comparison established a reason to look higher. In the higher range, 85–100% felt best to the designer on this departure. The exact rate and how it changes with zoom remain open.

**Finding across views.** The same 85 setting also looked okay in Follow 4×. The sky has to move visibly for the ship to feel like it is moving. A sky that slips only a little more slowly than the world can give enough separation. At 4× the inspector still moves the sky 85 logical pixels per 100 world units of camera travel, so this observation leaves the zoom response open.

The 1× Follow comparison used 45, 65, 85 and 100 logical pixels of sky travel per 100 world units of camera travel. The retained clip shows 85.

**Where this leaves the release.** The scroll comparison confirms a motion requirement and supplies a usable setting for the next example. It does not establish the appearance of the stars or the Milky Way. Four identifiable lights and the 85 setting can now serve as working conditions while judging appearance. The grey edge remains evidence about motion. No whole sky has been accepted for production.

<summary>02.7 / Which features of the real sky survive flight scale?</summary>

**Hypothesis.** A dark lane cutting through a fine field of starlight can read as a gap between distant stars even when individual faint stars merge. The earlier wash lacked the surrounding star texture that could give a dark lane this meaning.

The comparison used the photographic sky and the production white discs on the same Practice departure. The clip shows the photograph at pan 65.

[Retained panorama](/studies/distant-flight-sky-2026-11-18/milky-way.jpg) · [Photograph and credit](https://www.eso.org/public/images/eso0932a/)

**Designer’s finding.** Even smudgy, pixelated, blurry and too horizontal, the photographed Milky Way worked better than the previous examples, with one exception: the repetitive fog in 02.1 of the earlier, stopped study. The 65 pan setting also worked better on the photograph than it had on the grey goo. Judge the sky’s appearance and its travel together. This comparison leaves the preferred rate for the eventual artwork open.

**Question raised by the flight.** What survives the blur in this photograph that the procedural guesses missed?

**Appearance feedback.** The imagined treatments looked like something the designer could have drawn in GIMP. Repetition alone did not explain that reaction. The photographic reference remains useful despite its blur and rigid horizontal composition.

**Agreed reading.** A dark pocket or small bright patch gives the eye something to follow past the ship. This helps explain why the photographed sky carries motion at a pan setting that felt weaker on the grey wash.

**Emerging art target.** Keep those pockets and patches legible within the sweep of the Milky Way. Let individual stars show through. Within the established 2–5 recognizable lights, perhaps a couple could read as closer. A gleam is one possible way to give them presence. The nearer impression and the gleam are proposals. Judge them during the coast and the next burn, with the photograph as the visual reference.

<details id="near-pair">
<summary>02.8 / Two suns in front of the star field</summary>

**Question.** Can two of the four recognizable lights feel closer than the Milky Way, and what does a brief gleam contribute when the Wasp coasts?

**Hypothesis.** If two lights slide across a dark pocket a little faster than the pocket travels, the eye can place them in front of it. A brief gleam might make that separation easier to catch before the engine takes the eye again.

The retained B1 Follow flight shows two lights travelling 25% farther than the photographic plane at pan 65. One gleam peaks at 2.4 seconds during the coast; the left engine burns at three seconds.

**Study decision.** The nearer stars with gleam “work 110%” on this flight. Retain their relative travel and the two staggered gleams as the reference. The designer dislikes the round cores and left open whether round still works best at flight scale.

## 02.9 / The light between gleams

**Question.** What shape should a nearer star hold in the quiet interval between its gleams?

**Hypothesis.** A narrow core with two tapered ends might read as a distant point of light even before it gleams. At flight scale those tips could also make the star look like a small game icon. The round core is the reference for judging that tradeoff.

The comparison varied the two nearer cores between round and tapered, holding their filled area, colour, travel and gleam fixed. The clip shows the tapered proposal.

**Scope of this comparison.** The round cores bothered the designer, who also left open whether they serve the sky well. Tapered was proposed in response. No shape decision was requested or reached. This flight can expose a difference in the two cores; it cannot tell us which qualities of the photographed Milky Way the release sky needs to keep. Core shape can remain provisional while that larger choice is open.

## 02.10 / Where do many stars become a field?

**18 November 2026.** The designer observed that the study was drifting. Asking for a treatment of the Milky Way turned the problem into image-making before we had discussed how Void Drift draws starlight. Part 01 now asks how an individual light, a gathering of stars and an unresolved field read together during flight. The optical cross is an open possibility for the nearer stars.

**Evidence carried forward.** 02.1 exposed competition with the foreground. 02.2 and 02.5 exposed the limits of an unexplained wash. 02.3–02.4 established a small recognizable population. 02.5–02.7 showed why the structure and travel of a sky belong together. 02.8 established the nearer pair with gleam. 02.9 supplies a shape comparison with no decision.

**Hypothesis.** If many small grains gather around the same dark pocket, the pilot might first see a sweep of light and then recognize the stars making it. A continuous field could carry the pocket more clearly through a coast but lose that second reading.

**Final comparison.** Grains and Field sampled the same panorama at the same locations. Grains concentrated each sample into a small mark. Field spread its light across overlapping patches. Both recordings show B1 in Follow at pan 65 with the tapered nearer pair and gleam. Neither treatment was selected. [Panorama by ESO/S. Brunier](https://www.eso.org/public/images/eso0932a/)
