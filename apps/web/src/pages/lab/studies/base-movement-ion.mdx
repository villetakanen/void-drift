<h2 id="problem">01 / Momentum worth learning to control</h2>

Start at rest, fire the engines, then release them. How quickly should the ship gather
speed, how should acceleration change as speed builds, and what should happen when
thrust stops? These establish the basic experience of movement before turning and
recovery can be judged. The successful alpha gives us a playable foundation; its current
tuning is a starting point for investigation, not a settled straight-flight design.

This study should settle the basic relationship between engine input, turning,
acceleration and coasting. Two controls must support both a beginner's deliberate change
of course and a practiced player's flowing manoeuvres. Momentum, mass and engine
direction should explain what happens, while responsiveness and recoverability guide
where the game simplifies physics. Turning response, braking effort and behaviour at
speed remain questions to investigate together, rather than independent numbers to tune.

Ion is the simple default engine. Light supplies the baseline; Heavy counts as 1.75
Light engines. That relationship must become meaningful in flight; it does not yet
assign every mass, energy or speed value the same multiplier. Their established bodies
and beam effects carry into the trials. Gravity and other engine types are excluded.
Hull redesign, camera redesign and survival-resource balancing are outside this movement
decision.

### What success looks like

- **Easy to start:** a new player can understand the two controls and deliberately change course.
- **Movement makes sense:** the ship carries momentum, and the player can understand how firing an engine changes its path.
- **Mistakes are recoverable:** overshooting gives the player something to correct, without a long struggle to regain control.
- **Practice pays off:** anticipating turns leads to smoother, more accurate flying.
- **Both ions work:** Light feels dependable; Heavy's 1.75-Light contribution is useful and understandable without making flight unmanageable.
- **Flying is fun on its own:** accelerating, turning and drifting invite another go, even without gravity or survival pressure.

Playable trials will test these criteria through changing course and recovering from
overshoots, with both ion variants. Human play review must judge clarity, control and
enjoyment; simulation checks can verify the underlying rules but cannot establish fun.

The first investigation establishes acceleration, speed and coasting with Light ion. The
shared fourteen-second input sequence also exposes how side engines change the path; it
does not select a new turning model. Heavy remains a later scaling comparison.

Acceleration means the contribution of one aligned Light engine at rest, on a fixed

Side thrust uses the real Fighter mount angles and turn-response calculation; aft
engines fire only when both controls are held. The aft-only ship is deliberately
artificial: real builds require side engines. The constant-speed reference ignores all
inputs.

The bench reuses the production engine impulse: speed-increasing thrust tapers toward
the chosen powered-speed limit, while braking and transverse response remain available.
This is the current candidate to inspect, not an accepted movement rule. Travel momentum
persists on release; rotation uses the current direct response. There is no gravity,
drag, energy depletion, wrapping or randomness. Simulation uses 60 ticks per second with
four substeps. Distance is accumulated path length, not displacement. Route framing and
graph scales were fixed within each run and fitted to its settings.

Look for how engine count changes acceleration, how quickly each ship approaches the
reference speed, and what happens through single-engine input and release. These traces
expose the rules and their consequences; they do not yet establish whether free flight
is fun.

Review of 02.1 establishes engine direction and summed engine strength as the basis for
a semi-physical movement model. Relatively high acceleration per engine with a low speed
ceiling produced the most interesting path shapes in human review. No exact parameter
pair was recorded; this is a promising region to investigate, not final tuning or proof
of playable handling. How engine strength determines the speed ceiling remains open.

<h3 id="ceiling-comparison">02.2 / How much speed should combined force buy?</h3>

Combined directional force is the chosen basis for acceleration and the powered speed
ceiling. Compare the original example ships, plus two aft engines to make alignment
directly comparable with the side pair. All use the same Fighter geometry, effective
mass, Light engines and fourteen-second sequence from 02.1.

<figure>
  <img src="/studies/base-movement-ion/02-2-flat-ceiling.png" alt="Speed, ceilings, distance and paths with no extra-speed allowance" width="1120" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>14-second comparison with B = 0: a flat powered ceiling.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="/studies/base-movement-ion/02-2-ceiling-bonus.png" alt="Speed, ceilings, distance and paths with a 0.4 extra-speed allowance" width="1120" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Same run with B = 0.4; acceleration, base ceiling and falloff are unchanged.</figcaption>
</figure>

During LR, one aft supplies 1 equivalent, two aft supply 2, two angled side engines
supply about 1.80, and both pairs supply about 3.80. Sideways forces cancel in the
mirrored pair. One combined force vector drives each powered ship; its magnitude
determines acceleration and the ceiling. Aft engines fire only on LR; side engines
follow their respective controls. The artificial side-pair reference instantly travels
at half its two-side LR ceiling while either control is held, following its facing. One
button turns at half the current Fighter turn rate; LR holds heading. Release preserves
travel velocity and stops rotation. This replaces the original constant-speed reference
for turning review.

The candidate curve is V(E) = V₁ × [1 + B × (1 − exp(−k × (E − 1)))] for E ≥ 1. V₁ is
the one-Light ceiling, B the extra-speed allowance, and k the falloff rate. B = 0 gives
every powered arrangement the same ceiling. Larger B gives extra force more speed
benefit; larger k reaches that allowance with fewer engines. The stills compare B = 0
and B = 0.4 with acceleration and V₁ fixed.

Solid lines show speed and dashed lines show powered ceilings. A zero ceiling means no
active force, not braking: coasting preserves velocity, and a lower ceiling never clamps
existing speed. The graphs and readouts distinguish traversed length from position. The
accepted exploration defaults were 175 acceleration per engine, 250 one-Light speed, 0.4
extra-speed allowance and 0.8 falloff. Review confirmed this directional-force approach
works; these defaults carry forward into the turning study.

The question is whether two aft engines deserve a noticeable speed advantage over the
angled side pair, beyond their stronger acceleration, and how much advantage four
engines should gain. Fixed effective mass isolates that question; equipment mass, Heavy
scaling and free-flight feel remain later investigations. Current Fighter turning and
the production thrust taper remain the baseline.

The previous comparison of installed, active and directional counting addressed the
wrong question and is [preserved separately](/lab/studies/archive/ion-ceiling-rules); it
established no accepted rule.

Angular acceleration uses force × lever arm divided by inertia. The study uses a uniform
rectangle enclosing the real Fighter hull, measured about the existing ship origin, as a
shared inertia proxy. Equipment mass distribution and production's direct turn-response
gain are not included. A provisional 120°/s maximum bounds spin in every mode.
Stabilizers provide physical counter-torque, not passive damping; braking tapers on the
last substep so it cannot reverse spin. They do not auto-brake during LR. With no free
fall, release stops spin immediately, so stabilizers only assist commanded turns.

The artificial side-pair reference instantly sets travel to half its LR powered ceiling
while either button is held and turns at half the chosen maximum rate on a single
button. Release preserves its velocity and stops its spin immediately. It ignores the
choice of rotation mode and stabilizers, providing a fixed comparison.

The third graph shows signed angular speed; positive is clockwise on the canvas. Compare
the one-second releases after L and R: does continued spin create useful movement, and
does counter-thrust bring it back under control? Below one Light equivalent, the speed-
ceiling curve extends linearly to zero so a stabilizer alone has a defined positive
ceiling. Existing momentum is still never clamped. These are study-local mechanics; no
production flight, gravity or friction settings change.

<h2 id="integration">04 / Bring the accepted design into flight</h2>

The integration below is implemented for 0.4.2 in [the shared builder](/hangar) and
[Flight Lab](/hangar). The preserved 02 examples describe their investigation-time
conditions; their production-pending statements are historical. Post-integration flight
balance remains open.

1. Define validated ion tuning and optional stabilizers in shared flight/loadout schemas. Preserve version-2 builds with an off default; new builds use two Light-ion side engines.
1. Sum ion force vectors and torque at each simulation substep. Use net force for the saturating speed ceiling, retain external overspeed, integrate free-fall angular velocity, and cap automatic braking at zero. Budget stabilizer energy before applying force.
1. Promote the three accepted slit placements and shared Light-ion flames to the build renderer. Draw flight effects above hull and fittings. Preserve the exploratory examples as references, including the Heavy-ion Fighter and five-engine Cruiser.
1. Add the optional stabilizer switch to builder and Flight Lab. Lab trials use the real two-Light-ion loadout, expose the provisional ion values, and export/import those settings. Keep scored stock trials separate from these unranked experiments.
1. Verify directional summation, Heavy strength, coasting, counter-spin, power exhaustion, schema compatibility and deterministic replay. Inspect real rendering during L, R, LR and release/fade on each chassis. Run repository gates before the authorized 0.4.x release.

The motion reference is 14 one-second inputs: LR, LR, none, L, none, LR, none, R, none,
R, none, LR, LR, LR; start at rest without randomness. The Fighter flight stills use the
example’s follow camera at 2.4×. Integration checks use the actual game camera and
gravity, so trajectories are not expected to duplicate the isolated bench.

Human flight testing after integration must settle acceleration/speed balance and check
