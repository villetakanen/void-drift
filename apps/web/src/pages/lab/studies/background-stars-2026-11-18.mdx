## Outcome / Stopped in exploration

The designer closed the study as failed. It did not establish a starfield that feels like part of the game world. Two observations carry forward from the previews. Moving a single sky plane at a different pace from the arena gives the scene depth, especially during widening. Comparing whole fields in the same scene showed the need for structure across the sky.

The field's composition, the camera relationship in play, and how pilots discover the 6% Sun-like population are unresolved.

</section>

<section>

## 01 / Fires in the Sky

The flight background still uses an early field of white discs varied in size and opacity. For the planned 0.7.0 release, *Fires in the Sky*, the distant stars can help the game suggest a world beyond the arena. The field should have enough variety to reward looking at it as you fly, while leaving the ship, nearby bodies and flight cues easy to read.

The release brings plasma engines and weapons into play, and Sun-like stars carry its theme into the background. Hold the study’s G-class share at **6% of the generated field**, drawing on the Milky Way estimate. Their presence should come through over a stretch of flight, as a family of stars you notice here and there. Individual views can contain few or none. What gives them their character depends on how they look in the company of the rest of the field. [NASA, ESA and STScI’s stellar population comparison](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/comparison-of-g-k-and-m-stars-for-habitability/).

### Hi-fi low-fi starlight

Treat the browser as the machine we write the demo for. Draw simple Canvas marks or cached stamps and use the browser's scaling and blending to shape their light. Period games such as *R-Type* and *Gradius III* inspire us to ask how much character a small mark can carry. Pinpricks, soft edges and optical crosses are starting points for a contemporary treatment. Explore colour, brightness, footprint and falloff across the field, then see which differences make the Sun-like stars discoverable in flight. [Rendering method](/lab/render-model).

### Conditions for comparison

Use the flight renderer's world-anchored field as the reference. Keep its stable placement and full-stage coverage. Compare open dark space and views near bright flight effects at **1×, 2×, 4×, 8× and 16×**. The Planet Inspector maps these to **0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 8 CSS pixels per world unit**. The first four scales also match the current Speed camera, and 16× gives the study a closer inspection stop. [Planet Inspector](/lab/planet-inspector).

Compare the current white-disc field and complete proposed fields along the same camera path. Within each matched view, use the same point positions and G-class assignments across proposals. At extreme wide views the production renderer can switch to a separately seeded coarser grid, so match that grid too if it appears. Use a sequence with enough G-class stars to judge how they emerge in flight, including stretches where none are in view. Nearby stellar bodies belong to the separate [Stellar characters study](/lab/studies/stellar-characters-2026-11-11).

<details id="sky-scroll-planes">
<summary>02.2 / The sky as a scroll plane</summary>

The Genesis had independently scrolling display planes. The exterior stars in *Descent* used a different shortcut: the renderer turned far-off points with the view while leaving camera translation out of their positions. Both techniques give the sky its own relationship to the camera. [Sega technical overview](https://segaretro.org/images/1/18/GenesisTechnicalOverview.pdf), [Descent exterior star drawing](https://github.com/videogamepreservation/descent/blob/master/MAIN/ENDLEVEL.C#L1090-L1131).

Both clips show the same four-second pan at a fixed zoom. The foreground is a still from the game's World layer. The browser moved both images with CSS transforms. [CSS transforms](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Transforms).

### Independent scroll works

The slower sky read as a distinct, more distant part of the scene. The World HUD also looked better with the sky moving separately. The study keeps independent scroll as a direction. The one-fifth rate and the star cloud are conditions of this example. The scroll rate and the sky’s response to zoom remain open.

In flight, the camera can drive the offset of a prepared image and the browser can move it with a transform. A seamless repeat or a larger image with overscan could keep the stage covered. Coverage through zoom and arena wraps, visible repetition and compositing cost still need checking in the game.

</details>

<details id="sky-zoom">
<summary>02.3 / What the sky does when the camera zooms</summary>

The arena cuts from **1× to 2× to 4×**, then widens smoothly. That follows the shape of the Speed camera's inward cuts and outward easing. Both sky options keep the same slow pan. **Fixed sky** keeps the stars the same size. **Gentle zoom** lets the sky respond, reaching **1.41×** at the tightest arena view. The scales here are relative to the opening image. The arena is a flat capture, so its labels scale with it in this study.

### What the zoom showed

Moving a single sky plane at a different pace from the arena gave this preview a sense of depth. The widening stood out as the scene pulled back. This is a finding about relative motion in the preview.

The sky still feels separate from the game world. How the field belongs in the scene, how the Sun-like stars appear in it, and how the sky responds to the camera are open design questions.

</details>

### The field needs structure

Switching between the two skies made the texture of the background legible as a design choice. The sky needs structure across the field. That could come from a change in the density of small stars, a dark gap, or faint light from stars too distant to resolve. The shape and strength of that structure are open.
