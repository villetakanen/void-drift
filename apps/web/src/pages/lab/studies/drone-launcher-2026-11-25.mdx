## 01 / Develop the problem

The drone launcher turns potential into small autonomous craft carrying fission
payloads. A drone pursues an enemy and explodes nearby, or returns to the
launcher to be converted back into energy. Manufacturing takes time, and only
a few drones can be in play at once. The weapon's character comes from this
whole cycle: preparation aboard the ship, independent flight, and either an
explosion or recovery.

The launcher and drone already have their visual direction and approximate
proportions. The study brings those forms into working detail and explores how
the cycle should move and read in flight. How does the launcher communicate its
preparation? What makes the drone's pursuit and return convincing? How do the
pilot's movement and distance from the enemy shape the encounter?

The [original SVG](/studies/drone-launcher-2026-11-25/01-ion-weapons-source.svg)
and reference image preserve the artwork. The open rails, cyan planes, lime
fittings and pointed drone silhouette carry its identity. Mastering needs to
resolve construction, attachment, the drone's position in the launcher, and
release geometry while retaining that character. Exact world dimensions and
how much of the fitting remains visible on a hull need examination at flight scale.

The launcher belongs to the same equipment family as the fission engine. Their
explosions share the nuclear color: in the drone's close attack and in the
engine's plume. The [fission light study](/lab/studies/fission-light-2026-11-11)
provides a related visual reference. The drone itself has an ion drive with a
small plume, giving its travel a different light from its detonation.

### Manufacture, attack and recovery

The launcher acquires targets like other weapons. When it has a target within
range, it starts manufacturing a drone from potential. After a manufacturing
delay, it launches the drone with its fission payload. Deployment is limited
by the number of drones already in play: three is the starting point, with a
higher limit still possible.

An airborne drone heads towards an enemy while that enemy is in range. It
explodes when close enough. Without an enemy in range, it heads back towards
the firing ship if that ship is in range. At the launcher, the returning drone
is converted back into energy. This recovery is what “reload” means in the cycle.
The amount of potential used to manufacture a drone and the energy recovered
on return remain to be established.

These range conditions describe different moments: acquiring a launch target,
pursuing an enemy, returning to the ship, and detonating nearby. Their distances,
measurement origins and relationship need definition. The ship-range condition
also needs clarification: does it govern starting the return or beginning
recovery on arrival? The behaviour when neither destination is in range is open.

### Flight and machinery

The ion drive supplies thrust; turning and rotation control are still unresolved.
A small rotation stabilizer is one possibility. The movement needs to establish
how the drone points towards its destination, accelerates, handles drift and
overshoot, and meets a launcher that is itself moving. Departure velocity,
turning authority, braking and gravity all affect that character.

The launcher and drone must make the cycle understandable at game scale.
Manufacturing, release and recovery need a visible relationship to the machinery.
The small ion plume should help explain flight, while the nuclear explosion
marks the attack. Several drones must remain readable alongside their carrier,
targets and other weapon effects.

### Open design questions

- **Timing and commitment.** How long does manufacture take, when is potential
  spent, and what happens if the target is lost before launch? What pace do
  successive launches give the weapon?
- **Capacity and recovery.** Is the active limit per launcher or per ship? How
  do manufacturing, returning and conversion count towards it? How much energy
  returns, and how quickly can that potential become another drone?
- **Pursuit and return.** What do the range conditions measure? How does a drone
  choose or change its target, and when does it commit to returning? What happens
  when neither an enemy nor its firing ship is in range?
- **Attack and interruption.** What proximity triggers detonation, what does the
  explosion affect, and can a drone be intercepted? What happens if its firing
  ship is lost?
- **Pilot control.** How do Auto/Off and emergency energy affect manufacturing
  and drones already airborne? Which states need explicit feedback beyond the
  machinery, movement and light?

The inquiry centres on one complete cycle, from target acquisition through
manufacture and flight to detonation or recovered energy. Its timing, motion
and visual expression should be considered together: the launcher's form needs
to explain what it does, and the drone's behaviour needs to make sense from the
moving ship that sent it.

### 02.1 / Launcher and drone masters

The first inspection holds both objects still. Their paths, proportions, plane
colours and overlaps come from the original SVG, including the drone's small
blue gradient. Both are shown at the same scale so the drone keeps its size
relative to the launcher.

<MasterSpecimen />

The masters collect each object into its own SVG. Nested translations, scales
and reflections are flattened into paths, with named groups for the rails,
housing, receiver bands and drone faces. Unused definitions, other equipment,
labels and editor metadata are removed. The drone's scattered pieces are brought
together in their original paint order, retaining its single exhaust gradient.

Both coordinate frames point nose-up, with the front at zero and the horizontal
silhouette centred on zero. They use the same source drawing units. These are
artwork origins, not chosen mount points. The launcher has 26 faces in 14 groups;
the drone has seven faces in seven groups.

Inspect the rail spacing, rear assembly, drone silhouette and their relative
size against the draft. This step establishes the static masters for review;
manufacturing, release, steering, recovery and explosion remain to be explored.

### 02.3 / Eject, coast, ignite

The launch presentation and starting values were accepted as reasonable for
continuing the study: 17 u launcher length, 68% attachment, unchanged master
proportions, +70 u/s ejection and 32 u unpowered distance. Launches occur at
1, 3, 5 s and so on. The next drone appears only during the final 0.10 s.

The drone starts at the yellow receiver and follows the moving, turning rails
until its centre reaches the muzzle. At that crossing it inherits both the
carrier’s translation and the muzzle’s sideways velocity from rotation. Coast
distance includes rail travel and is measured relative to that inherited drift;
ignition cannot precede muzzle clearance. With these values, ignition is at
approximately 1.46 s. The original blue gradient remains World artwork; a
separate shared ion plume appears on FX when powered.

### 02.4 / Accepted Fighter motion and plume

The designer selected **Fighter** motion for continuing the study: the production
Fighter’s stabilized turn response, with its 180°/s ceiling and canted thrust.
Releasing a turn counter-burns the remaining rotation without reversing it;
lateral velocity remains. The comparison used repeating left, forward, right,
forward commands, each for 0.5 s relative to ignition. Wasp cut its main drive
while turning; Scout doubled the turn response. Fighter was selected from these
three study adaptations; no production drone handling was adopted.

The retained values are **60 u/s²**, the **271 u/s engine envelope**, and the
**50% Service Ion → Light Ion plume**. The engine envelope tapers acceleration
along world velocity rather than clamping inherited speed; braking and transverse
thrust remain available. The midpoint plume has a 40.8 u full-output source length,
shrinking with the square root of delivered output through the shared FX surface.
The original blue gradient remains separate World artwork, including when the
main drive is off.

### 02.5 / Pursuit, return and drift

One drone now flies autonomously against one moving Scout target. Launch is
allowed only while an enemy is **within 450 u of the carrier**. In flight,
an enemy strictly inside **500 u of the drone** takes priority: pursue it.
Otherwise return towards the carrier if it is strictly inside **500 u of the
drone**. Outside both airborne ranges, switch off the main drive and drift
while the stabilizers settle rotation.

These distances remain suspect and are deliberately adjustable: the menu has
separate launch, pursuit and return ranges. Centre-to-centre measurement is this
step’s working interpretation. Launch includes its boundary (≤450 u); airborne
checks exclude theirs (less than 500 u). Neither measurement origin nor these values
are production targeting decisions.

Each encounter substitutes one launch for the earlier repeating salvo so the
drone’s decisions remain readable. The carrier coasts at 40 u/s. The target
follows a staged smooth route with continuous position and velocity, using
production Scout artwork. Its route is an experiment condition, not enemy AI;
the drone chooses its own commands throughout.

The earliest release is 1 s. If the target is already in launch range, the
accepted seated preview begins at 0.90 s and release is at 1 s. If it enters
range later, the drone appears for 0.10 s before release. Losing range cancels
that preview; release checks range again. There is no manufacturing simulation
or queued launch after a target has left range.

At the default ranges, both encounters launch legitimately at 1 s and ignite
at about 1.47 s. **Long pursuit** pursues until about 8.83 s, then drifts through
14 s: the enemy is about 501 u away and the carrier 726 u away at the transition.
The main plume extinguishes; a remaining −30°/s rotation settles while the
roughly 81 u/s drift continues. **Early departure** switches from Pursuit to
Return at about 3.73 s. It approaches the moving carrier over the remaining
course, reaching about 3 u separation and 41 u/s against the carrier’s 40 u/s
at 14 s. This demonstrates approach and settling, not recovery.

Use 1× to inspect the whole encounter. Higher magnifications are close inspections
and can take objects outside the carrier’s view. The blue dashed circle shows
pursuit range; if return range differs, a yellow circle shows that second
radius. Distances, current command and spin distinguish the decision from the
inertia that follows it. Changing a range or encounter reconstructs the same
selected instant, so the new conditions are applied from the start.

The controller uses the pirate pilot’s bounded planning approach: eight
Off/Left/Right/Forward command sequences, a 1.2 s prediction, and a 0.3 s first
command followed by coasting or forward thrust. It looks again every eight
60 Hz ticks, immediately on destination changes. Its cached response table is
built from the accepted drone’s Fighter steering including starting angular
velocity. Each predicted impulse applies the drone’s actual 271 u/s envelope
to its current velocity. It borrows no pirate hull, standoff distance, hazard
avoidance or energy assumptions. Pursuit aims for the moving target’s centre;
Return also values matching carrier velocity. These are study choices to judge,
not settled drone intelligence.

The accepted launcher, rail attachment, ejection, coast, ignition, blue World
indicator and ion plume remain. The smallest yellow-family star is present,
with the carrier’s course outside its gravity field and no planets. The drone
has no gravity, drag, collision or attack in this step. Approaching the carrier
does not trigger recovery or energy conversion. Manufacture, capacity, target
selection among several enemies and detonation remain later questions.
